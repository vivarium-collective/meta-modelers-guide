Q Once a single cell divides (Fig 10-1), can the emergence of multicellularity be expressed as compositional topology rewrites — DEVELOPMENT, where cells reorganize into a colony that is itself a higher-level composite with aggregate observables (Fig 10-2), and EVOLUTION, where variation and selection extend the interface alphabet itself by adding a new port to a lineage (Fig 10-3)?

Claim

Multicellularity is two compositional topology rewrites at a level above the single cell: DEVELOPMENT reorganizes cells into a colony that is itself a composite with its own aggregate behavior, and EVOLUTION rewrites a population under selection so a lineage gains an entirely new interface port.

If division makes two cells from one, multicellularity is what those cells do next — and it arrives as two distinct compositional rewrites. DEVELOPMENT (Fig 10-2) treats a biofilm as a composite one level above the single cell: cells adhere to a surface (attached 0 → 1.35, adhesion 0 → 0.67) and grow (cells 1 → 1.57), secrete a shared extracellular matrix (ecm 0 → 1.8), and the whole assembly acquires aggregate, colony-level observables that no single cell has — an emergent biofilm_mass accumulating 0 → 2.25. The compositional point is that the same modelling apparatus that describes one cell describes their collective by nesting: the colony is a store containing cells, with processes acting on the group. EVOLUTION (Fig 10-3) is the slower rewrite. A population of variants competes under selection — the population grows (cell_ecoli.cell_count 1 → 3.4) — and, crucially, a variant lineage acquires a *new interface port*, a capability emerging from 0 → 0.57 (cell_O157.new_port). This is the compositional signature of innovation: the interface alphabet itself is extended, not merely re-valued. Two multicellular rewrites, one modelling idea — reorganize the topology, and (for evolution) grow the alphabet of ports.

Compose cells with attachment, ECM secretion, and growth behind the Fig 10-2 development interface, and competing variants under shared resource and dilution behind the Fig 10-3 evolution interface, and run both to completion through the engine.

Conclusion

Claims

  • Multicellularity is two compositional topology rewrites. DEVELOPMENT: the colony grows (cells 1→1.57), attaches (attached 0→1.35), and secretes ECM (0→1.8), and colony-level observables emerge from nesting cells in a store — an aggregate biofilm_mass accumulating 0→2.25 that no single cell has. EVOLUTION: under selection the population grows (cell_ecoli.cell_count 1→3.4) and a variant lineage acquires a new interface port (cell_O157.new_port 0→0.57), extending the interface alphabet itself.

Evidence

  • COLONY-GROWS — biofilm_mass 0 → 2.25; ecm 0 → 1.8; cells 1 → 1.57, attached 0 → 1.35
  • POPULATION-GROWS — cell_ecoli.cell_count 1 → 3.4 under selection
  • NEW-CAPABILITY — cell_O157.new_port 0 → 0.57 on a variant

Limitations

  • Toy-real colony and selection: growth, ECM accumulation, population growth and new-port emergence are real in shape, but rate constants, selection coefficients and timing are illustrative.

Next steps

  • Give the colony spatial structure to test nutrient gradients and detachment as compositional processes, and introduce multiple competing new ports to test selection over a richer interface-innovation space.

Plan & assumptions

Limitations

What this study explicitly does NOT validate (prevents overclaim).

  • DEVELOPMENT is a well-mixed colony with mild growth (cells 1→1.57); no spatial gradients, detachment, or nutrient-limited zones within the biofilm. Adhesion and ECM-secretion rates are illustrative, not fitted to a measured biofilm.
  • EVOLUTION is a two-variant competition with a single new port; real evolution explores a combinatorial space of interface changes not sampled here. Selection coefficients and mutation timing are illustrative, not inferred from a measured fitness landscape.
