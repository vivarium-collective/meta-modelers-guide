Q Is the paper's orchestration claim (Fig orchestration, panel a) executable rather than merely drawn -- does a runnable process-bigraph composite in which two processes update at DIFFERENT rates through ONE shared store actually reproduce multi-timescale coordination: the fast process filling the shared pool far more often than the slow one converts it, the two rates coordinated through the shared store, and the combined trajectory reflecting both timescales while conserving mass?

Claim

The paper's multi-timescale orchestration claim (Fig orchestration, panel a) is executable, not merely drawn: composing a fast process and a slow process through ONE shared `pool` store, each updating on its own interval, reproduces panel (a)'s "processes that update at different rates through a shared store" as a runnable, deterministic trajectory. Over a fixed 20-tick run the fast process updates the shared store on every tick (20 changes) while the slow process converts only 3 times, `molecules` is a sawtooth (rises on the fast clock, drawn down at each slow firing), `biomass` advances monotonically in a coarse staircase on the slow clock (levels 0 -> 3.0 -> 7.2 -> 11.88, never returning to the pool), and mass is conserved through the shared store across both timescales -- at every tick `molecules + biomass` equals the cumulative fast injection (== elapsed time), ending 8.12 + 11.88 = 20.0. The combined trajectory reflects both timescales at once; the composite is didactic and uncalibrated, so this demonstrates the ORCHESTRATION MECHANISM, not any biological magnitude.

This is the runnable realization of the paper's §Composition-vs-orchestration distinction (main.tex §composition vs orchestration; Fig orchestration, panel a). The paper defines orchestration as *when* processes act and *how* their updates are combined across timescales -- distinct from composition, which is *how* processes are coupled through shared variables. Panel (a) states it directly: "Multi-timescale orchestration coordinates processes that update at different rates through a shared store and discrete-event scheduling." (Panels (b) workflow-DAG and (c) event-driven graph rewrites are structural/schematic and are deliberately NOT part of this runnable demonstration -- only panel (a), the piece a minimal run can show honestly.) The fig02-runnable composite (`meta_modelers_guide.composites.fig02-runnable`) wires two processes to one shared `pool` store, each on its own clock: * `FastProduction` (interval 1.0, rate 1.0) adds to `pool.molecules` every base tick -- dmolecules/dt = +rate. * `SlowConversion` (interval 5.0, yield_frac 0.6) fires once per 5 ticks, converting a fraction of whatever has accumulated in the pool into `pool.biomass` and drawing that same amount back out of `pool.molecules`. Run together on the process-bigraph engine's discrete-event scheduler, the two clocks interleave and the combined trajectory reflects BOTH timescales: `molecules` ramps up on the fast clock (+1 per tick) and saws down each time the slow process fires, while `biomass` advances in a coarse staircase on the slow clock -- flat between slow firings, stepping up at each. Over the pinned 20-tick run the fast pool updates on every tick (20 changes) while the slow store changes only 3 times (the three visible conversions at t=10, 15, 20), so the fast process touches the shared store far more often than the slow one -- the multi-timescale signature made concrete. Crucially the two are coordinated through the ONE shared store and mass is conserved across the timescales: the fast process is the sole source and the slow process only MOVES mass between `molecules` and `biomass` within the pool, so at every emitted tick the shared-store total `molecules + biomass` equals the cumulative fast injection, which for rate=1.0 equals the elapsed time exactly (t=20: 8.12 + 11.88 = 20.0). The whole trajectory is deterministic -- no RNG anywhere -- so every number here reproduces exactly run to run.

Test

Run the `fig02-runnable` composite (FastProduction interval 1.0 rate 1.0 and SlowConversion interval 5.0 yield_frac 0.6 wired to one shared pool store) to completion over 20 ticks through the process-bigraph engine, and measure whether two processes updating at different rates through a single shared store reproduce Fig orchestration panel (a): the fast clock outpacing the slow clock, a sawtooth fast pool, a coarse monotone biomass staircase, and mass conserved through the shared store across the two timescales.

Result

Over the deterministic 20-tick run, the fast process updates the shared store on every tick (20 changes) while the slow process converts only 3 times (at t=10, 15, 20), so the fast clock visibly outpaces the slow clock. `molecules` is a sawtooth -- it ramps +1/tick and is drawn down at each slow firing (9->7 at t=10, 11->7.8 at t=15, 11.8->8.12 at t=20) -- while `biomass` advances monotonically in a coarse staircase on the slow clock (0 -> 3.0 -> 7.2 -> 11.88), never returning to the pool. The two rates are coordinated through the ONE shared store and mass is conserved across the timescales: at every emitted tick `molecules + biomass` equals the cumulative fast injection (== elapsed time), ending 8.12 + 11.88 = 20.0. Every value is fixed and deterministic (no RNG), so the whole trajectory reproduces exactly run to run. The paper's multi-timescale orchestration claim (Fig orchestration, panel a) is thus executable, not merely drawn -- demonstrated here at the didactic (uncalibrated) level for the orchestration mechanism, with panels (b) workflow-DAG and (c) event rewrites left schematic.

✗ If primary tests fail:
Diagnose:
  • the fast pool does not outpace the slow store (the two intervals are no longer distinct, or the scheduler is not honoring per-process intervals)
  • molecules is not a sawtooth (the slow process is not drawing mass back out of the pool, or the fast process stopped filling)
  • biomass is not a monotone staircase (the slow process is returning mass to the pool, or firing on the wrong clock)
  • molecules + biomass no longer equals elapsed time (a source or sink was introduced -- mass is being created or lost across the timescales)
Block downstream: any future study reusing the multi-timestepping-through-a-shared-store pattern to demonstrate orchestration

Conclusion

Claims

  • The paper's multi-timescale orchestration claim (Fig orchestration, panel a) is executable, not merely drawn: composing a fast process (interval 1.0) and a slow process (interval 5.0) through ONE shared pool store reproduces panel (a)'s "processes that update at different rates through a shared store" as a runnable, deterministic 20-tick trajectory. The fast process updates the shared store on every tick (20 changes) while the slow process converts only 3 times; molecules is a sawtooth (rises +1/tick, drawn down at each slow firing); biomass advances monotonically in a coarse staircase on the slow clock (0 -> 3.0 -> 7.2 -> 11.88), never returning to the pool.
  • The two rates are coordinated through the one shared store and mass is conserved across the timescales: at every tick molecules + biomass equals the cumulative fast injection (== elapsed time), ending 8.12 + 11.88 = 20.0. The combined trajectory reflects both timescales at once, and nothing is created or lost in crossing them.

Evidence

  • FAST-CLOCK-OUTPACES-SLOW-CLOCK -- fast pool changes 20 times, slow store 3 times (20 > 3*3)
  • FAST-POOL-IS-A-SAWTOOTH -- molecules rises +1/tick and drops at t=10, 15, 20
  • BIOMASS-ADVANCES-MONOTONICALLY-ON-SLOW-CLOCK -- staircase 0 -> 3.0 -> 7.2 -> 11.88, monotone, exactly 3 conversions
  • MASS-CONSERVED-ACROSS-TIMESCALES -- molecules + biomass == time at every tick; final 8.12 + 11.88 == 20.0
  • FAST-PROCESS-ADDS-EACH-TICK -- unit control, +1.0 per unit interval
  • SLOW-CONVERSION-CONSERVES-WHAT-IT-MOVES -- unit control, delta biomass + delta molecules == 0

Limitations

  • This composite is didactic / toy-real: the rates, yield fraction, intervals, and run length are hand-picked to make the two timescales legible, not calibrated to any biological process or literature target. It demonstrates the orchestration MECHANISM, not a biological magnitude.
  • Only panel (a), multi-timestepping through a shared store, is demonstrated at runtime. Panels (b) workflow-DAG and (c) event-driven graph rewrites are structural / schematic in the paper and are not part of this runnable composite; this study makes no runtime claim about them.

Next steps

  • A future study could make panel (b) executable by wiring a small dependency DAG of Steps and asserting execution follows the dependency structure, and panel (c) by running an event-driven rewrite (division / engulfment / bursting) that mutates the composite structure mid-run.

Plan & assumptions

Key assumptions

  • This `fig02-runnable` composite is **didactic / toy-real**: a minimal illustrative demonstration built to make Fig orchestration panel (a) play forward under test, NOT calibrated to any literature. `FastProduction` (rate 1.0), `SlowConversion` (yield_frac 0.6), the intervals (1.0 and 5.0), and the 20-tick run length are hand-picked magnitudes chosen to make the two timescales legible in one short run, not fitted to any named biological process, rate, or measurement. The quantities `molecules` and `biomass` are abstract shared-store values, not calibrated concentrations or masses.
  • Only panel (a) -- multi-timestepping through a shared store -- is demonstrated at runtime. Panel (b) (workflow orchestration as a dependency DAG) and panel (c) (event-driven graph rewrites -- division, engulfment, bursting) are structural / schematic in the paper and are deliberately NOT part of this runnable composite. This study makes no runtime claim about (b) or (c).
  • The composite is fully deterministic -- there is no RNG in either process nor in the scheduler -- so every value pinned here (the final split, the biomass staircase levels, the shared-store conservation) reproduces exactly run to run. The exact numeric pins are regression pins over the current process configs and intervals; the directional claims (fast outpaces slow, sawtooth, monotone staircase, conservation) are the a-priori predictions that hold for any such fast/slow pair.
  • Orchestration timing here is the process-bigraph engine's own discrete-event scheduler acting on each process's declared `interval`. The slow process's effect becomes visible in the emitted `biomass` series one coarse step after each firing window; the ``interval`` fields, not any hand-written schedule, drive when each process acts.
